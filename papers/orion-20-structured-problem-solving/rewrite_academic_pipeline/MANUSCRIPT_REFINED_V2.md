# Closure Obstruction Does Not Identify a Unique Missing Method

## Abstract

When a solver fails because its operation language is closed under transformations that cannot reach the target, it is tempting to infer that the system must invent a particular missing method. That conclusion is generally too strong. We formalize a closure-relative obstruction and show that reachability, minimal language expansion, and primitive indispensability are distinct claims.

Let \(L\) be a set of admitted operations and \(\operatorname{Cl}(L)\) the states or functions reachable under their allowed composition. A target outside \(\operatorname{Cl}(L)\) is unreachable by every solver restricted to \(L\). This establishes the need for some expansion, but not which expansion is uniquely correct. In a finite Boolean construction, the target
\[
T_p(x,y,z)=p(x,y)\oplus p(x,z)\oplus p(y,z)
\]
lies outside the affine closure, yet either AND or OR alone yields a singleton minimal expansion. Neither primitive is indispensable. The two expansions are extensionally sufficient through different identities, and their difference cancels inside the target.

A planned 480-case, four-domain empirical successor executed zero scientific cases because its donor, task, evaluator, and verifier inputs were not content-bound. It contributes no evidence for automated method invention.

The paper's result is a negative theorem with operational consequences: a closure witness can justify expansion or abstention, but it cannot by itself authorize a unique invented method. Claims of method discovery require an additional identification argument that excludes alternative minimal expansions.

## 1. Introduction

A failed solver can be limited by data, search, representation, or the operations it is allowed to compose. The last case is especially attractive for theories of scientific method invention. If the target lies outside the closure of the current language, then no amount of search inside that language can succeed.

The obstruction is real, but the usual interpretation overreaches. From
\[
t\notin\operatorname{Cl}(L)
\]
one may infer that the current language is insufficient. One may not infer that a particular primitive \(a\) is uniquely necessary. Several incomparable expansions can make the target reachable, and different expansions can be minimal under different cost or admissibility criteria.

This paper separates four propositions:

1. the current language cannot reach the target;
2. an expanded language can reach it;
3. an expansion is minimal;
4. a particular primitive is indispensable.

Only the first follows directly from closure obstruction.

## 2. Closure-relative reachability

Let \(S\) be a state or function space and \(L\) a set of admitted primitives. Define \(\operatorname{Cl}(L)\) as the smallest set containing the initial objects and closed under the compositions licensed by \(L\).

**Proposition 1 (closure obstruction).** If \(t\notin\operatorname{Cl}(L)\), then no procedure whose every construction step uses only operations from \(L\) can produce \(t\).

The statement is immediate from the definition of closure, but it performs an important diagnostic role. It distinguishes a search failure from a language failure. More compute cannot repair an unreachable target while the operation set remains fixed.

The proposition is relative to the declared closure. If the implementation already contains an operation not represented in \(L\), or if the closure omits an allowed composition, the diagnosis is invalid. Closure membership is therefore a formal contract, not a metaphor for difficulty.

## 3. Expansion, minimality, and indispensability

An expansion \(E\) is sufficient for target \(t\) when
\[
t\in\operatorname{Cl}(L\cup E).
\]

It is inclusion-minimal when no proper subset of \(E\) is sufficient. A primitive \(a\) is indispensable only when every admissible sufficient expansion contains \(a\).

These properties are not equivalent.

- Closure obstruction implies that the empty expansion is insufficient.
- A sufficient expansion establishes reachability.
- Inclusion-minimality says only that no member of that expansion can be removed.
- Indispensability quantifies over all admissible expansions and is therefore much stronger.

A method-invention claim that names a unique missing primitive needs the final property or an explicit alternative identification criterion.

## 4. Finite Boolean counterexample

Consider an affine Boolean language \(L_{\mathrm{aff}}\) generated by XOR, constants, and variable projections. Its closure contains the affine Boolean functions.

For a binary predicate \(p\), define
\[
T_p(x,y,z)
=
p(x,y)\oplus p(x,z)\oplus p(y,z).
\]

Choose a target whose truth table is non-affine. Then
\[
T_p\notin\operatorname{Cl}(L_{\mathrm{aff}}),
\]
so Proposition 1 certifies that the affine language is insufficient.

Now admit either of two nonlinear primitives.

1. Add AND.
2. Add OR.

Each singleton expansion is sufficient for the target in the registered construction. Each is inclusion-minimal because the target is outside the affine closure. Yet neither primitive is indispensable because the other singleton expansion also works.

The reason is structural. AND and OR differ by affine terms:
\[
x\lor y = x\oplus y\oplus (x\land y).
\]
When the binary predicate is inserted into the three-pair XOR template, the additional affine contributions cancel. The target therefore identifies a nonlinear equivalence class, not a unique primitive.

## 5. What the counterexample rules out

The example refutes the inference:
\[
\text{closure obstruction}
\Longrightarrow
\text{unique missing primitive}.
\]

It does not refute all method identification. A unique method can be selected by adding further constraints, for example:

- a cost model;
- a restricted admissible primitive catalogue;
- invariance or locality requirements;
- intervention evidence distinguishing competing constructions;
- a theorem that every sufficient expansion contains a common primitive;
- a downstream responsibility that one expansion satisfies and another does not.

The point is that those conditions must do the identification work. They cannot be inferred from reachability alone.

## 6. A safe invention protocol

A closure-based solver can use the following bounded protocol.

First, certify that the target is outside the current closure. Second, enumerate or characterize admissible expansions. Third, test sufficiency under the declared semantics. Fourth, distinguish inclusion-minimal expansions from globally optimal or indispensable ones. Fifth, report multiplicity whenever more than one minimal expansion survives.

When the expansion set cannot be searched or the target semantics are not verified, the correct outcome is `CANNOT_CHECK`, not an invented primitive chosen by plausibility.

This protocol changes the role of the obstruction. It authorizes a need for expansion, but it does not grant scientific authority to a particular expansion.

## 7. The unexecuted empirical successor

A broader successor was specified across four domains, nine arms, three seeds, and 480 planned cases. It was intended to test whether a bounded invention policy could recover withheld operations under external task and verifier control.

No scientific case executed. The required donor objects, task instances, evaluator gold, and verifier bindings were absent or not content-bound. A planned grid is not evidence, and acquisition failure is not a negative performance result.

The empirical method-invention claim therefore remains untested. The present paper stands on the exact closure and non-uniqueness result.

## 8. Relation to prior work

Program synthesis, inductive logic programming, theorem proving, automated discovery, search over DSLs, and algebraic closure all contain mature accounts of expressive insufficiency and language expansion. The paper does not claim closure reasoning or Boolean functional bases as new.

Its residual contribution is the claim discipline around method invention: an explicit separation of obstruction, sufficiency, minimality, and indispensability, together with a finite counterexample showing that a clean closure witness can coexist with non-unique minimal expansions.

## 9. Limitations

The Boolean construction is deliberately small. It establishes logical non-entailment, not the frequency of non-unique expansions in natural scientific domains. The closure model assumes exact semantics. Approximate targets, stochastic operations, and learned representations require a tolerance or probabilistic extension.

The paper does not show that method invention is impossible. It shows that one commonly proposed certificate is insufficient to identify a unique invention.

## 10. Conclusion

A target outside the closure of the current operation language proves that more search inside that language cannot succeed. It does not name the missing method. In the finite Boolean construction, AND and OR are distinct singleton minimal expansions for the same target, so neither is indispensable. Closure obstruction should therefore trigger expansion search, comparison, or abstention, not unilateral scientific promotion of one primitive. A method-discovery claim requires an identification argument beyond reachability.
